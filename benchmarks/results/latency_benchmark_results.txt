cmarket Latency Benchmark Results
=================================

Overview
--------

This benchmark measures the latency characteristics of core cmarket
matching-engine operations as the active order book grows.

The goal is to evaluate not only average performance, but also tail
latency and scaling behavior under increasingly large in-memory order
sets.

Measured operations:

- Limit order placement
- Single-order matching
- Order cancellation
- Order modification
- Multi-level matching

Book sizes:

- 100 active orders
- 1,000 active orders
- 10,000 active orders
- 100,000 active orders

Latency statistics:

- p50 / median
- p95
- p99
- worst observed latency


Benchmark Methodology
---------------------

Build configuration:
Release

Platform:
Apple Silicon ARM64

Timing source:
std::chrono::steady_clock

Measured samples per operation:
1,000

Warmup iterations per operation:
100

Setup and replenishment work is performed outside the timed region
where possible.

For mutating operations, the benchmark maintains an approximately
constant active-book size between measurements instead of rebuilding
the entire matching engine for every sample.

The multi-level benchmark constructs ten distinct executable price
levels and submits a market order large enough to consume all ten.
This ensures the workload measures true multi-level traversal rather
than multiple fills at a single price level.

Engineering Findings
====================

Order Placement
---------------

Order placement remains the lowest-latency operation at the largest
tested book size, with a median latency of approximately 47.6 us at
100,000 active orders.

However, placement latency still increases substantially with book
size.

One contributor is duplicate OrderId validation in OrderManager,
which currently searches the active-order vector before insertion.

The results indicate that order placement is not constant-time with
respect to active-order count.


Single-Order Matching
---------------------

Median single-order matching latency grows from approximately 1.8 us
at 100 active orders to approximately 271.9 us at 100,000 orders.

The matching engine can determine whether an incoming limit order
crosses the aggregate book using the best bid or ask, avoiding an
unnecessary full scan for non-crossing orders.

Once an order actually crosses, however, the engine still searches
active orders to identify the best resting match.

This active-order lookup becomes increasingly expensive as the book
grows.


Cancellation
------------

Cancellation exhibits the strongest relative scaling degradation in
the benchmark.

Median latency grows from:

208 ns at 100 orders

to:

166.6 us at 100,000 orders.

The active-order container is currently vector-based. Cancellation
therefore involves locating the target order and erasing it from the
vector, which may also require moving subsequent elements.

The measured results make cancellation lookup and removal a clear
candidate for future data-structure optimization.


Modification
------------

Median modification latency grows from:

375 ns at 100 orders

to:

260.8 us at 100,000 orders.

Modification performs more work than a simple in-place field change.
It must maintain matching semantics, order-book consistency, FIFO
priority rules, and exception guarantees.

The current implementation may perform order lookup, removal,
aggregate-book adjustment, matching, reinsertion, and state
preservation work during the operation.

This combination becomes increasingly expensive as the active order
set grows.


Multi-Level Matching
--------------------

Multi-level matching has the highest absolute latency of the measured
operations.

At 100,000 active orders:

p50:
approximately 1.98 ms

p99:
approximately 2.18 ms

worst observed:
approximately 2.27 ms

A multi-level execution repeatedly locates and consumes resting
liquidity.

Because locating the next matching order still depends on searching
the active-order collection, that lookup cost is repeated across
multiple fills.

This makes the matching data structure one of the highest-impact
areas for future performance work.


Tail-Latency Behavior
=====================

At 100,000 active orders, p99 latency remains relatively close to p50
for most operations.

Examples:

Place:
47.6 us p50 -> 58.6 us p99

Match:
271.9 us p50 -> 282.8 us p99

Cancel:
166.6 us p50 -> 177.7 us p99

Modify:
260.8 us p50 -> 276.0 us p99

Multi-level match:
1.98 ms p50 -> 2.18 ms p99

This suggests that much of the observed large-book latency comes from
repeatable algorithmic work rather than rare latency spikes.

Worst-case observations can still be influenced by operating-system
scheduling, cache effects, and other system noise, so percentile
measurements provide a more useful basis for comparing operations.


Performance Engineering Conclusions
===================================

The benchmark identifies four primary sources of scaling cost in the
current architecture:

1. Linear active-order lookup

   Several matching and order-management paths still search the
   active-order vector.

2. Vector erase and movement

   Cancellation and operations that remove active orders may require
   movement of subsequent vector elements.

3. Repeated matching lookup

   Multi-fill executions repeat the matching search for each resting
   order consumed.

4. State-management overhead

   Modification and other operations maintain strong correctness and
   exception-safety guarantees, which introduce additional work on
   mutation paths.


A hash-based active-order index was previously evaluated as a possible
optimization.

Although it improved asymptotic lookup behavior, the measured
end-to-end performance improvement was small relative to the added
state-management complexity, so it was not retained.

This benchmark therefore serves as measurement-driven evidence for
future architectural changes rather than assuming that a theoretically
faster data structure automatically produces a meaningful system-level
improvement.

Summary
=======

cmarket was benchmarked across active order books ranging from
100 to 100,000 orders using latency distributions rather than only
average throughput.

At 100,000 active orders:

- Limit placement p99:       58.6 us
- Single-order match p99:   282.8 us
- Cancellation p99:         177.7 us
- Modification p99:         276.0 us
- Ten-level match p99:        2.18 ms

The results show that the engine performs efficiently on small books,
while vector-based active-order management and repeated matching
searches become increasingly important at larger scales.

The strongest relative scaling degradation occurs in cancellation,
while multi-level matching has the highest absolute latency.

These measurements provide a concrete performance baseline for future
work on indexed order lookup, cancellation efficiency, price-level
organization, and multi-fill execution.
